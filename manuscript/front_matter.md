# How much does assumed enzyme zonation matter for predicted acetaminophen hepatotoxicity? A stability analysis against measured human profiles

## Highlights

- Measured human enzyme zonation is systematically shallower than the gradients prior models imposed.
- Replacing those gradients cuts predicted pericentral acetaminophen adducts ~90% at the therapeutic dose.
- At overdose the replacement is null — glutathione saturation, not zonation, governs predicted injury.
- A stability test separating robust from assumption-dependent predictions of a zonated toxicity model.

## Abstract

Acetaminophen hepatotoxicity is spatially restricted to the pericentral zone because the enzymes of its metabolism are zonated along the hepatic sinusoid, and successive zonated models have captured this by imposing qualitative, literature-derived gradients for the enzyme rates, ultimately traceable to a rodent-derived review (Gebhardt 1992). Here we ask a stability question rather than make a recalibration claim: which conclusions of such a model survive replacing the assumed gradients with measured human profiles? We re-derived per-enzyme abundance folds from single-cell spatial proteomics (from the binned intensity profiles, not the published regression coefficients, which are not fold changes), imposed them on a 16-hepatocyte model with total enzyme conserved, and propagated isoform and protein–mRNA uncertainty by Monte Carlo. At the therapeutic dose (4 g) the measured gradients reduce predicted pericentral adduct burden by ~90% (90% CI −97 to −81%), a result robust to the full uncertainty range; at overdose (16 g) the same replacement has no significant effect (median −4%, CI −20 to +11%), because glutathione saturation, not zonation, controls the prediction. The assumed gradients therefore systematically overestimate pericentral injury at low dose, but are irrelevant at high dose. The stability test itself is the transferable contribution: it separates robust from assumption-dependent predictions of a zonated toxicity model, and applies directly to the still-open questions of saturable kinetics and transport coupling.
